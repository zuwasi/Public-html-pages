The problem · 02

A crypto inventory tells you what you have. It does not tell you what to fix first

Q.security's platform answers discovery: what cryptographic assets exist, where they are, how quantum-vulnerable they are. The quarter's engineering plan is the next question, and it is combinatorial

What a ranked list gives you

Sort by score, work down the list

Rank systems by exposure, take the top ones that fit the budget. This ignores two things a real migration always has:

  • Dependencies: the customer portal cannot move until the shared edge gateway terminates hybrid key exchange. The top-ranked item drags its prerequisite in.
  • Budget interactions: with more (or less) budget, the best set changes, not just its size.
What a wave planner must answer

Which wave is provably best, and why

A migration wave is a subset of systems that fits the engineering budget and respects prerequisites. The planner must return the subset that minimizes exposure under the clock, and be able to prove no other feasible subset does better.

That is an optimization problem with a certificate, not a dashboard view.

The asset · 03

One asset, walked down from the business: a payment flow running on pre-quantum cryptography

The chain Q.security's platform inventories, and the clock that makes it urgent: harvest now, decrypt later

Retail Banking → Payments → PKI → TLS termination: ECDHE P-256 + RSA-2048 certificate (business unit) (application) (platform) (component: HNDL-exposed key exchange; HNSL integrity-asset certificate)
The clock

Harvest now, decrypt later

Traffic recorded today is kept confidential by the ECDHE P-256 key exchange, and a cryptographically relevant quantum computer breaks that exchange: anything recorded now can be decrypted later. The RSA-2048 certificate is a second clock, not the first one: it authenticates the connection, and its signatures can be forged later (harvest now, sign later). Every asset with data that must stay secret that long is already losing its protection, year by year, while the migration runs.

The paper behind the demo

Gupta & Mittal 2026

"Post-quantum readiness and cryptographic transition planning for enterprise cloud" (Cybersecurity 9:147) turns the clock into arithmetic: an expected exposed fraction E[f] of each asset's hackable lifetime, driven by a Q-day distribution and the migration schedule. This demo uses the paper's midline model end to end (PDF in the repository root).

The six candidate migrations in this demo are modeled on the enterprise target profile Q.security addresses: retail bank, payment flows, PKI, network crypto, identity
The model · 04

The Mosca arithmetic: every year of delay is exposure you cannot recover

Expected exposed fraction E[f] against the migration start year: the paper's model, integrated exactly and cross-checked by Monte Carlo

Two readings off the curve

Starting the migration in 2026 leaves E[f] = 0.366 of the exposure window open. Waiting until 2030 raises it to 0.539: nearly half of every asset's hackable lifetime spent readable to a future CRQC.

The Python module integrates the piecewise-linear CDF exactly. An independent 200,000-sample Monte Carlo in Wolfram agrees to 1.5·10⁻³, about 1.4 standard errors of the estimate (SE ±0.0011; the run's assertion gate is 3 SE = 0.0034).

The method · 06

Six projects is 64 possible waves. Enumerate all of them

Small instances must be solved exactly, so the quantum layer and the proof layer have a ground truth to be checked against

for mask in 0..63: # one bit per project cost = sum of weeks of selected projects value = sum of exposure points feasible = (cost ≤ budget) and (PRT selected → GWY selected) exact optimum = argmax value over feasible masks ranked list = greedy by points-per-week, pulling prerequisites in
Exact

Exhaustive enumeration

64 plans is small enough to check completely, in Python and again in Wolfram. The optimum is a fact, not an estimate.

Realistic baseline

The naive exposure-ranked list

The simplest prioritizer: walk the systems ranked by exposure-per-week, take each one whose prerequisite chain fits the remaining budget.

Quantum layer

Same instance, 6 qubits

The penalized cost becomes a diagonal Hamiltonian on 6 qubits; QAOA samples the wave space. The classical enumeration is the oracle the quantum result is judged against.

Implementation: data/plan_search.py asserts the story properties and writes data/results/plans.json; the Wolfram and Lean stages verify against that file

Results · 08

Four extra weeks do not grow the wave. They change which systems get fixed

Budget 16 (the live event): the optimum flips to the gateway+portal wave, and no 12-week-feasible plan reaches it

The flip is a theorem, not an observation: Lean proves every 12-week-feasible plan is worth at most 70, and the 16-week optimum is worth 92. The naive ranked list cannot even see the flip: it happens to reach 92 at 16 weeks, but it loses 4 points at budgets 10 and 11, 6 at the default 12, and 1 at 14.

MantiQ 1a · Wolfram · 09

The wave plan as a 6-qubit cost Hamiltonian

One qubit per candidate: the penalized cost is diagonal, so QAOA's phase layer encodes the whole planning problem

What the Wolfram pipeline checks

  • Encoding: C(z) = −(points) + 12·max(0, weeks − budget) + 20·[PRT without GWY], computed for all 64 states.
  • Penalties preserve the optimum: the penalized argmin equals the constrained argmin at both budgets (checked, not assumed).
  • Z decomposition: 56 non-identity Pauli-Z terms of 64 (57 including the identity), max weight 6, verified to reconstruct the cost table exactly.
  • Landscape: the dense p=1 expectation over (gamma, beta); the search-found grid optimum is marked. The search is honest dense-grid plus local refinement, not a proof of global optimality.

Platform artifact · 14

From local simulation to platform artifact: the Classiq QMOD

The demo runs quantum simulation locally by design; the same cost layer is written as an SDK-valid QMOD model a cloud platform can synthesize

What was generated

qwave_planner.qmod

The verified Walsh-Hadamard Z decomposition (56 non-identity Pauli-Z phase terms, max weight 6; the identity term is global phase and is dropped) as a Classiq QMOD phase layer written by the Classiq SDK's own pretty-printer, with the search-found p=1 angles (gamma = 0.0412, beta = 2.8579) and the same 6-qubit encoding.

A Python SDK driver (synthesize, execute 4096 shots, compare against the Wolfram prediction P(opt) = 0.0604, P(feasible) = 0.5069) ships next to it, marked as requiring a Classiq account. It was not run in the demo: the quantum evidence here is local and reproducible.

Scaling the approach

What changes past 6 projects

  • Enumeration stays the decision layer at this size; beyond roughly 30 candidates exact methods hand over to integer programming or dynamic programming, not to heuristics.
  • Lean moves from decide to real proofs of the encoding, and QAOA's contribution stays empirical: measured behaviour on each instance, no advantage claim.
  • Real deployments feed CBOMs from the discovery platform instead of a hand-built instance, and re-plan every quarter as the clock moves.
classiq/generate_qmod.py regenerates the QMOD from wolfram/qwave_planner.json through the Classiq SDK's write_qmod, so the artifact traces to the verified decomposition; its built-in self-check reproduces the Wolfram p=1 distribution to TVD 4.4·10⁻¹³
